The super Ihara zeta and its two lattice states, from the ground up

A tutorial that starts with a graph and four by four matrices and ends at the statement "the Riemann hypothesis for a graph with a flux is an invariant vacuum of a Gaussian circuit acting on the syndromes of a GKP code". Every number on this page is computed live from the graph you pick; nothing is quoted.

The route has four parts. A builds the Ihara zeta function of a regular graph and its Riemann hypothesis (the Ramanujan property) from walks and eigenvalues. B rewrites one non-backtracking step as a linear map on a phase space with two coordinates per vertex, and finds a conserved quadratic form whose positivity is exactly the Riemann hypothesis. C adds a sign on every edge, which splits the walks into bosonic and fermionic ones; the ratio of their zeta functions is the super Ihara zeta, and its zeros come from the fermionic modes. D explains GKP states and shows that the fermionic sector contains two lattice states: a qubit encoded on the cycle lattice of the graph, and a torus of syndromes on which the step acts, whose periodic points count the points of an elliptic curve.

Status. This page explains the record in notes/adelic-gkp/graph-ihara.md, graph-super.md and lattice-tower.md (2026-10-05). Those are records, not reviewed results: nothing is registered in the claims database and no REFUTE review has run. Section 17 lists each statement with its status. Standard facts (Ihara–Bass, Kesten–McKay, Heilmann–Lieb, Deligne) are standard; the GKP readings are the record's readings.

Part A · 1A graph and its matrix

A graph is a finite set of vertices with some pairs joined by edges. Every graph on this page is regular: every vertex has the same number of neighbours, which we write as \(q+1\). The number \(q\) will be the "size of the field" in everything that follows, so keep an eye on it. For the tetrahedron \(K_4\), every vertex has three neighbours, so \(q = 2\).

The adjacency matrix \(A\) records the graph: \(A_{ij} = 1\) if vertices \(i\) and \(j\) are joined, otherwise \(0\). It is symmetric, with zeros on the diagonal, and every row sums to \(q+1\).

Demo 1The running examplePick a graph. Every later demo in Parts A and B uses this choice.

Two of the examples are special. The cube is bipartite: its vertices split into two groups with every edge running between the groups; that gives it an extra trivial eigenvalue later. The prism \(C_{21}\times K_2\), a ring of 21 squares, is the one example that will fail the Riemann hypothesis; it is there so that failure can be watched.

Part A · 2Vectors on vertices, eigenvalues, and closed walks

A vector here is just a number attached to each vertex, \(f = (f_1,\dots,f_n)\). The matrix acts by summing over neighbours: \[ (Af)_i = \sum_{j\ \text{adjacent to}\ i} f_j . \] An eigenvector is a vector that \(A\) only rescales, \(Af = \lambda f\); the number \(\lambda\) is its eigenvalue. Because \(A\) is real and symmetric, there are \(n\) real eigenvalues (counted with multiplicity) and the eigenvectors can be chosen orthogonal, so any vector is a sum of eigenvectors.

Two eigenvalues are always present and we call them trivial. The constant vector \((1,1,\dots,1)\) has eigenvalue \(q+1\), the degree. If the graph is bipartite, the vector that is \(+1\) on one side and \(-1\) on the other has eigenvalue \(-(q+1)\). All other eigenvalues are called non-trivial. Apply \(A\) over and over to a random vector and the constant component wins, because \(q+1\) is the largest eigenvalue in absolute value: that is the power iteration in the demo.

Traces count closed walks. The entry \((A^k)_{ii}\) counts walks of \(k\) steps that start and end at vertex \(i\), because each step chooses a neighbour. So the trace \(\operatorname{Tr}A^k = \sum_i (A^k)_{ii}\) counts all closed walks of length \(k\), and since the trace of a matrix is the sum of its eigenvalues, \[ \#\{\text{closed walks of length }k\} = \operatorname{Tr}A^k = \sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_j^{\,k}. \] This is the whole idea of a zeta function in one line: a count of closed orbits on one side, a sum of powers of eigenvalues on the other.

Part A · 3Non-backtracking walks and the operator \(B\)

Ordinary closed walks are a poor analogue of prime numbers, because most of them are padding: step to a neighbour, step straight back. The right notion is a non-backtracking walk, one that never immediately reverses an edge. Closed non-backtracking walks that also do not reverse at the closing step are the "periodic orbits" of the graph.

To count them we need a matrix on directed edges. Each edge \(\{a,b\}\) gives two directed edges \(a\to b\) and \(b\to a\), so a graph with \(|E|\) edges has \(2|E|\) of them. The non-backtracking operator (Hashimoto operator) is \[ B_{e,f} = 1 \quad\text{if the end of } e \text{ is the start of } f \text{ and } f \text{ is not the reverse of } e,\qquad\text{else } 0 . \] Then \(N_k = \operatorname{Tr}B^k\) counts closed non-backtracking walks of length \(k\), each counted once per choice of starting edge. \(B\) is not symmetric, so its eigenvalues can be complex; that is where the "critical circle" will come from.

A prime cycle is a closed non-backtracking walk that is not a repetition of a shorter one, counted up to the choice of starting point (and with the two directions counted separately). Writing \(\pi_\ell\) for the number of prime cycles of length \(\ell\), each contributes \(\ell\) walks (the \(\ell\) starting points) to every \(N_k\) with \(\ell \mid k\): \[ N_k = \sum_{\ell\,\mid\,k} \ell\,\pi_\ell . \] For \(K_4\): four triangles times two directions give \(\pi_3 = 8\), and \(N_3 = 24\).

Part A · 4The Ihara zeta function and the Ihara–Bass formula

Package the prime cycles into one function of a variable \(u\), exactly as the Riemann zeta packages the primes into a product over \(p\): \[ \zeta_X(u) = \prod_{\text{prime cycles } C} \frac{1}{1-u^{\ell(C)}} . \] Taking the logarithm and expanding each factor as a geometric series turns the product into a sum over all closed walks, prime or not, and gives \[ \log\zeta_X(u) = \sum_{k\ge1}\frac{N_k}{k}\,u^k = \sum_{k\ge 1}\frac{\operatorname{Tr}B^k}{k}u^k = -\log\det(1-uB), \] so \(\zeta_X(u) = 1/\det(1-uB)\): the zeta function is the inverse of a characteristic polynomial, and its poles are the reciprocals of the eigenvalues of \(B\). (The last equality is the identity \(\log\det = \operatorname{Tr}\log\).)

The matrix \(B\) is \(2|E|\times2|E|\), but the Ihara–Bass formula reduces everything to the \(n\times n\) adjacency matrix: \[ \det(1-uB) = (1-u^2)^{|E|-|V|}\,\det\!\big(1 - Au + q u^2\big). \] The factor \((1-u^2)^{|E|-|V|}\) is the "tree term": it only knows the number of independent cycles \(g = |E|-|V|+1\). All the structure sits in \(\det(1-Au+qu^2)\), which factors over the eigenvalues of \(A\): \[ \det(1-Au+qu^2) = \prod_{j=1}^n \big(1-\lambda_j u + q u^2\big). \] So each eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of \(A\) contributes one quadratic factor, hence two poles of \(\zeta_X\).

4b · A proof of Ihara–Bass by vectorisation

The formula looks like a miracle: a determinant on \(2|E|\) directed edges collapses to one on \(n\) vertices. Here is a proof that uses nothing beyond matrix multiplication and one identity about determinants, and that explains the exponent \(|E|-|V|\). It also proves the formula for an arbitrary graph, with the degree matrix \(D\) in place of \(q+1\). The idea is to stop thinking of edge functions as a list and think of them as matrices.

Step 0: the edge space is a vectorised matrix space. Let \(V = \mathbb C^n\) be the vertex space. A vector in \(V\otimes V\) has components \(\psi_{ab}\) indexed by ordered pairs of vertices, which is the same data as an \(n\times n\) matrix \(\Psi\) with entries \(\Psi_{ab}\): this identification of \(V\otimes V\) with matrices is vectorisation, and we use it freely. A directed edge \(a\to b\) is an ordered pair with \(A_{ab} = 1\). So the edge space is \[ \mathcal E = P_A\,(V\otimes V),\qquad P_A\Psi = A\circ\Psi\ \ (\text{entrywise product}), \] the matrices supported on the pattern of \(A\). The projector \(P_A\) is diagonal in the pair basis, with the vectorised adjacency matrix down its diagonal: it is the Schur multiplier by \(A\), and \(\dim\mathcal E = 2|E|\), one dimension per nonzero entry of \(A\). Three elementary operators on \(V\otimes V\) will do all the work:

  • the swap \(F\): \(\psi_{ab}\mapsto\psi_{ba}\), i.e. \(\Psi\mapsto\Psi^{T}\). It reverses every edge, and \(F^2 = 1\). Since \(A\) is symmetric, \(F\) commutes with \(P_A\).
  • the complete-graph adjacency \(K = J - 1\), where \(J = \mathbf 1\mathbf 1^{T}\) is the all-ones matrix, acting on the first factor as \(K\otimes 1\): it replaces the start vertex by any other vertex.
  • the pattern projector \(P_A\): keep what is an edge.

Step 1: the non-backtracking step is "reverse, re-aim, keep". For \(\psi\in\mathcal E\), \[ \big((K\otimes1)F\psi\big)_{ab} = \sum_{c}K_{ac}\,(F\psi)_{cb} = \sum_{c\ne a}\psi_{bc}, \] and after \(P_A\) only the pairs \(a\to b\) that are edges survive; the input vanishes off edges, so the sum runs over edges \(b\to c\) with \(c\ne a\). That is exactly the definition of \(B\) from Section 3. So, as an operator on \(\mathcal E\), \[ B = P_A\,(K\otimes 1)\,F,\qquad\text{in matrix language}\quad B(\Psi) = A\circ\big((J-1)\,\Psi^{T}\big). \] One line: the Hashimoto operator is the superoperator "transpose, multiply by the complete-graph adjacency, mask by the pattern".

Step 2: the all-ones matrix has rank one, so the step factors through the vertices. Write \(J\otimes 1 = (\mathbf 1\otimes 1)(\mathbf 1^{T}\otimes 1)\): a map \(V\otimes V\to V\) followed by a map \(V\to V\otimes V\). Composed with the projector these are the two lifts from Section 6, \[ \tau g = P_A(\mathbf 1\otimes g),\ \ (\tau g)_{a\to b} = g_b;\qquad \sigma f = P_A(f\otimes\mathbf 1),\ \ (\sigma f)_{a\to b} = f_a, \] so \(P_A(J\otimes1)P_A = \tau\tau^{T}\), while \(P_A(1\otimes 1)P_A = P_A\) is the identity of \(\mathcal E\). Hence, using \(F\tau = \sigma\) (reversing an edge swaps start and end) and therefore \(\tau^{T}F = \sigma^{T}\), \[ B = (\tau\tau^{T} - 1)\,F = \tau\,\sigma^{T} - F . \] This is the whole structure: propagate through the vertex space, minus the backtrack. The map \(\sigma^{T}\colon\mathcal E\to V\) sums an edge function over the edges leaving each vertex, and \(\tau\) spreads a vertex function onto the edges arriving there. The relations we need are read off in one line each: \[ F^2 = 1,\qquad \sigma^{T}\tau = A,\qquad \sigma^{T}\sigma = \tau^{T}\tau = D, \] where \(D\) is the diagonal matrix of degrees, \(D = (q+1)\,1\) for a regular graph: \(f^{T}\sigma^{T}\tau g = \sum_{a\to b}f_ag_b = f^{T}Ag\), and \(g^{T}\tau^{T}\tau g = \sum_{a\to b}g_b^2 = \sum_b \deg(b)\,g_b^2\).

Step 3: one determinant identity. For any \(X\colon V\to\mathcal E\) and \(Y\colon\mathcal E\to V\) (rectangular matrices), \[ \det\nolimits_{\mathcal E}(1 - XY) = \det\nolimits_{V}(1 - YX). \] Proof: the block matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}1_{\mathcal E}&X\\Y&1_V\end{pmatrix}\) has determinant \(\det(1_{\mathcal E})\det(1_V - Y1_{\mathcal E}^{-1}X)\) by eliminating the first block, and \(\det(1_V)\det(1_{\mathcal E} - X1_V^{-1}Y)\) by eliminating the second (Schur complements). This is Sylvester's identity. It is the step that trades the \(2|E|\)-dimensional space for the \(n\)-dimensional one.

Step 4: the computation. Take \(u\) with \(u^2\ne1\). Because \(F^2 = 1\), the operator \(1+uF\) is invertible with \[ (1+uF)^{-1} = \frac{1-uF}{1-u^2}, \] and its determinant is read off the eigenvalues of \(F\): the vectors \(e\pm\bar e\) show that \(F\) has eigenvalue \(+1\) on a space of dimension \(|E|\) and \(-1\) on one of dimension \(|E|\), so \(\det_{\mathcal E}(1+uF) = (1+u)^{|E|}(1-u)^{|E|} = (1-u^2)^{|E|}\). Now \[ \begin{aligned} \det\nolimits_{\mathcal E}(1-uB) &= \det\nolimits_{\mathcal E}\big(1 + uF - u\,\tau\sigma^{T}\big) = \det\nolimits_{\mathcal E}(1+uF)\;\det\nolimits_{\mathcal E}\!\Big(1 - \underbrace{u(1+uF)^{-1}\tau}_{X}\;\underbrace{\sigma^{T}}_{Y}\Big)\\ &= (1-u^2)^{|E|}\;\det\nolimits_{V}\!\Big(1 - \frac{u}{1-u^2}\,\sigma^{T}(1-uF)\tau\Big) \qquad\text{(Sylvester)}\\ &= (1-u^2)^{|E|}\;\det\nolimits_{V}\!\Big(1 - \frac{u}{1-u^2}\,(A - uD)\Big) \qquad\text{(since } \sigma^{T}\tau = A,\ \sigma^{T}F\tau = \sigma^{T}\sigma = D)\\ &= (1-u^2)^{|E|-|V|}\;\det\nolimits_{V}\!\big((1-u^2) - uA + u^2D\big) = (1-u^2)^{|E|-|V|}\;\det\nolimits_{V}\!\big(1 - uA + (D-1)u^2\big). \end{aligned} \] Both sides are polynomials in \(u\) and they agree for every \(u^2\ne1\), so they agree identically. For a \((q+1)\)-regular graph \(D - 1 = q\), which is the formula of Section 4. \(\blacksquare\)

What the proof says. The exponent \(|E|-|V|\) is \(|E|\) from the eigenvalues of the swap, minus \(|V|\) from pulling \((1-u^2)^{-1}\) out of the vertex determinant: the "tree term" is the part of the edge space that the vertex bottleneck cannot see, and \(B\) has \(|E|-|V|\) eigenvalues \(+1\) and \(|E|-|V|\) eigenvalues \(-1\) there, as Section 6 claimed. The two lifts \(\sigma,\tau\) are the same maps that gave the phase-space step \(M\) in Section 6: there we looked at what \(B\) does on \(\sigma V + \tau V\), here we take the determinant on all of \(\mathcal E\) and the complement contributes only \((1-u^2)\)-factors. Nothing in Steps 2 to 4 used that the entries of \(A\) are \(0\) or \(1\): with a signed matrix \(A_s\) in place of \(A\) (the signs attached to \(\tau\), so that \(\sigma^{T}\tau = A_s\) while \(\tau^{T}\tau\) is still \(D\)) the same lines give \(\det(1-uB_s) = (1-u^2)^{|E|-|V|}\det(1-A_su+qu^2)\), which is what Section 10 uses. The notebook's quantum Ihara–Bass theorem (shard 08) is this proof with the vertex space replaced by an operator space, itself a vectorised \(V\otimes V\), and \(\sigma^{T},\tau,F\) replaced by a start map, a propagation map and a backtracking map for an arbitrary family of superoperators; the Sylvester step is the same.

Part A · 5Zeros, the critical circle, and the Riemann hypothesis for graphs

Fix one eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of \(A\). Its factor \(1-\lambda u+qu^2\) vanishes at \(u = 1/\mu\) where \(\mu\) solves \[ \mu^2 - \lambda\mu + q = 0,\qquad \mu = \tfrac12\big(\lambda \pm \sqrt{\lambda^2-4q}\big). \] We call the two numbers \(\mu\) the lines of that mode (the word comes from spectroscopy: they are the frequencies that the counts \(N_k\) "emit"). Their product is \(q\). If \(\lambda^2 < 4q\) the square root is imaginary, the two lines are complex conjugates, and each has \(|\mu| = \sqrt q\). If \(\lambda^2 > 4q\) they are real, one bigger than \(\sqrt q\) and one smaller.

So all lines of a mode sit on the circle \(|\mu| = \sqrt q\) exactly when \(|\lambda| \le 2\sqrt q\). The trivial eigenvalue \(\lambda = q+1\) always violates this (its lines are \(q\) and \(1\): these are the "poles" of the graph zeta, the analogues of the pole of Riemann's zeta at \(s=1\) and its partner at \(s=0\)). The Riemann hypothesis for the graph is the statement that every non-trivial line lies on the circle: \[ \text{RH}(X):\qquad |\lambda| \le 2\sqrt q \ \text{ for every non-trivial eigenvalue } \lambda \text{ of } A . \] A graph with this property is called Ramanujan. Why \(2\sqrt q\)? Because the infinite \((q+1)\)-regular tree, the universal cover of every such graph, has spectrum exactly \([-2\sqrt q, 2\sqrt q]\) (Kesten–McKay). Ramanujan means: no eigenvalue outside the tree's band, so no mode grows faster than the tree allows.

Why is this the analogue of the Riemann hypothesis? For Riemann's zeta, write \(u = p^{-s}\) and the Euler factor is \(1/(1-p^{-s})\); the critical line \(\operatorname{Re}s = \tfrac12\) becomes \(|u| = p^{-1/2}\). For the graph, \(u = q^{-s}\) and the critical line is \(|u| = q^{-1/2}\), that is \(|\mu| = \sqrt q\). Same statement.

RH for the graph: all non-trivial lines on \(|\mu| = \sqrt q\), equivalently \(|\lambda|\le2\sqrt q\). The trivial mode is the pole pair \(q, 1\). The prism violates it; the others on this page satisfy it.

Part B · 6Phase space: two numbers per vertex

Everything so far lived on \(2|E|\) directed edges. There is a smaller space on which the non-backtracking step acts: pairs of vertex vectors. Given two vertex vectors \(f\) and \(g\), build an edge vector by putting \(f\) at the start of each directed edge and \(g\) at its end: \[ (\sigma f)(a\to b) = f_a,\qquad (\tau g)(a\to b) = g_b . \] Apply \(B\) and read off what happens (the computation is a few lines: \(B\sigma f\) at edge \(e\) sums \(f\) at the start of every allowed successor of \(e\), which is the end of \(e\), taken \(q\) times; \(B\tau g\) sums \(g\) over the \(q\) neighbours of the end of \(e\) other than the start of \(e\)): \[ B(\sigma f) = q\,\tau f,\qquad B(\tau g) = \tau(Ag) - \sigma g . \] So the \(2n\)-dimensional space spanned by \(\sigma f\) and \(\tau g\) is invariant, and on the pair \((f,g)\) the step is the block matrix \[ M = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ q & A \end{pmatrix},\qquad \begin{pmatrix} f \\ g\end{pmatrix}\mapsto \begin{pmatrix} -g \\ qf + Ag\end{pmatrix}. \] The eigenvalues of \(M\) are exactly the lines: \(\det(\mu - M) = \det(\mu^2 - \lambda\mu + q)\) over the eigenvalues \(\lambda\) of \(A\). The remaining \(2|E|-2n\) eigenvalues of \(B\) are \(\pm1\), the tree term.

Now the key observation. Think of \(f\) as a position coordinate \(x\) and \(g\) as a momentum coordinate \(p\), one pair per vertex: a classical phase space of \(n\) oscillators. Let \(\Omega\) be the standard symplectic form, \(\Omega\big((x,p),(x',p')\big) = x\cdot p' - p\cdot x'\), the oriented area in each \((x_i,p_i)\) plane. A direct computation gives \[ M^{T}\Omega M = q\,\Omega . \] The step multiplies every oriented area by \(q\). Dividing by \(\sqrt q\), the matrix \(S = M/\sqrt q\) is symplectic: an area-preserving linear map of phase space, which in quantum optics is a Gaussian unitary on \(n\) modes. The factor \(\sqrt q\) is the same \(\sqrt q\) as the critical circle.

For one mode (one eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of \(A\), after diagonalising \(A\)) the map is the \(2\times2\) matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\q&\lambda\end{pmatrix}/\sqrt q\), with determinant 1 and trace \(\lambda/\sqrt q\). A \(2\times2\) area-preserving map with \(|\text{trace}|<2\) is a rotation in suitable coordinates: orbits are ellipses. With \(|\text{trace}|>2\) it is a hyperbolic squeeze: orbits run off along a hyperbola. The threshold \(|\lambda/\sqrt q| = 2\) is the band edge again.

Part B · 7The Weil form: a conserved energy whose positivity is RH

An area-preserving map of the plane that rotates must preserve some ellipse, hence some positive quadratic form, an "energy". Which form does the step preserve? Compute: with \[ \mathcal Q = \begin{pmatrix} q & A/2 \\ A/2 & 1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathcal Q(x,p) = q\,|x|^2 + x\cdot Ap + |p|^2 , \] one checks \(M^T\mathcal Q M = q\,\mathcal Q\), so \(S^T\mathcal QS = \mathcal Q\): the normalised step conserves \(\mathcal Q\). (There is a cleaner formula: \(2\mathcal Q = \Omega\,(M - qM^{-1})\), the symplectic form composed with the step minus its "adjoint" \(qM^{-1}\). This is the Weil form of the explicit formula in disguise; the demo confirms the invariance numerically.)

On a single mode with eigenvalue \(\lambda\) the form is the binary quadratic form \(q x^2 + \lambda xy + y^2\), whose discriminant is \(\lambda^2 - 4q\). A \(2\times 2\) symmetric matrix with positive trace is positive definite exactly when its determinant \(q - \lambda^2/4\) is positive. Therefore:

Weil positivity for a graph. \(\mathcal Q\) is positive definite on the mode \(\lambda\) if and only if \(\lambda^2 < 4q\). It is positive on all non-trivial modes if and only if the graph is Ramanujan. On the trivial mode \(\lambda=q+1\) the determinant is \(-(q-1)^2/4 < 0\), so \(\mathcal Q\) always has exactly one negative direction there (two for a bipartite graph): this negative plane is the analogue of the "pole" terms in Weil's criterion for Riemann's zeta, which are also the only permitted negative contributions.

The number of negative directions of \(\mathcal Q\) is a clean diagnostic: it equals (number of trivial eigenvalues) plus (number of non-trivial eigenvalues outside the band). For \(K_4\), Petersen and \(K_5\) it is 1; for the cube 2; for the prism it is 5, because four non-trivial eigenvalues (\(2.911\) twice and \(-2.978\) twice) lie outside \(\pm2\sqrt2 = \pm2.828\).

Three things to notice. First, the form is written down before the spectrum is known: it is built from \(q\) and \(A\) only, the "arithmetic data". Second, it is not the obvious inner product on the edge space (\(B\) is not a normal operator there); it is the form singled out by the symplectic structure. Third, "the metric" that the notebook keeps looking for in the case of Riemann's zeta is, for a graph, just this \(\mathcal Q\): a Hilbert–Pólya inner product in which the step is unitary, which exists exactly when RH holds.

Keep

The non-backtracking step is a symplectic similitude \(M^T\Omega M = q\Omega\). It conserves the Weil form \(\mathcal Q = q|x|^2 + x\cdot Ap + |p|^2\). RH for the graph \(\iff\) \(\mathcal Q\) is positive on the non-trivial modes \(\iff\) the normalised step rotates every non-trivial mode instead of squeezing it.

Part B · 8The step as a circuit of three gates

The symplectic matrix \(S\) factors into three elementary pieces: \[ S = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-A&1\end{pmatrix}}_{\text{shear by }A}\ \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}}_{\text{quarter turn}}\ \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt q&0\\0&1/\sqrt q\end{pmatrix}}_{\text{squeeze}} . \] Check: squeeze first, \((x,p)\mapsto(\sqrt q\,x, p/\sqrt q)\); quarter turn, \((x,p)\mapsto(-p, x)\), which is the Fourier transform of a mode; shear, \((x,p)\mapsto(x, p - Ax)\), which couples neighbouring vertices. Multiply out and you get \(M/\sqrt q\). In quantum-optics language the three are a squeezer on every mode, a Hadamard (Fourier) on every mode, and a controlled-Z along every edge. The last two together are the circuit that prepares the graph state of \(X\) on qubits, and their product is an integer matrix, so it maps the lattice \(\mathbb Z^{2n}\) to itself. The squeeze is the only part that is not a lattice symmetry.

One consequence worth seeing now, because it is the graph version of the functional equation \(\xi(s) = \xi(1-s)\). With no edges (\(A = 0\)) the step is "squeeze, then quarter turn", and it squares to \(-1\): the Fourier transform turns a squeeze by \(\sqrt q\) into a squeeze by \(1/\sqrt q\), so two steps undo the squeezing. Reversing every directed edge (the symmetry \(e\mapsto\bar e\) that sends \(B\) to \(B^T\)) is the time reversal that implements \(\mu \leftrightarrow q/\mu\), that is \(s \leftrightarrow 1-s\).

Part C · 9Flux: a sign on every edge, and the double cover

Now the new ingredient. Give every edge a sign \(s_e = \pm1\). A walker picks up the product of the signs of the edges it uses; around a closed walk, that product is \(\pm1\) and depends only on which cycles the walk winds around. Think of \(-1\) as a half quantum of Aharonov–Bohm flux threading the cycle. Flipping all the signs at one vertex (a gauge change) changes no closed-walk product, so the physical data is the signing modulo gauge: this is what we call a flux. For a graph with \(g = |E|-|V|+1\) independent cycles there are exactly \(2^g\) fluxes; for \(K_4\), \(g = 3\) and there are 8.

A flux is the same thing as a double cover \(\hat Y\) of the graph: take two copies of every vertex, \((v,+)\) and \((v,-)\), and join \((a,\pm)\) to \((b,\pm)\) for a positive edge, \((a,\pm)\) to \((b,\mp)\) for a negative one. Swapping the two copies is a symmetry \(\tau\) of \(\hat Y\) with \(\tau^2 = 1\). For \(K_4\) with every edge negative the double cover is the cube.

Functions on \(\hat Y\) split into two sectors under \(\tau\):

  • Bosonic (even) sector, \(\tau f = f\): these are functions on \(Y\) itself, and \(A\) acts on them by the plain adjacency matrix \(A\).
  • Fermionic (odd) sector, \(\tau f = -f\): these are functions on \(Y\) too, but \(A\) acts by the signed adjacency matrix \(A_s\), with entries \(\pm1\). A walker in this sector has antiperiodic boundary conditions around every cycle of odd flux.

The signed matrix \(A_s\) is still symmetric, so it has real eigenvalues, the fermionic eigenvalues; they are not the eigenvalues of \(A\). Whether they lie in the band \([-2\sqrt q, 2\sqrt q]\) is a new question, asked flux by flux. The same construction on directed edges gives a signed non-backtracking operator \(B_s\) (the sign of the edge being entered), and \(\operatorname{Tr}B_s^k\) counts closed non-backtracking walks weighted by their sign.

Part C · 10Bosons, fermions, and the super zeta

Each sector has its own Ihara zeta: \(1/\det(1-uB)\) for the bosons and \(1/\det(1-uB_s)\) for the fermions. The super zeta is their ratio, \[ Z_s(u) = \frac{\det(1-uB_s)}{\det(1-uB)} = \frac{\det(1-A_su+qu^2)}{\det(1-Au+qu^2)} , \] the tree factors \((1-u^2)^{|E|-|V|}\) being identical in the two sectors and cancelling. Its zeros come from the fermionic eigenvalues (the numerator) and its poles from the bosonic ones (the denominator). This is exactly the shape of the zeta function of a curve over a finite field, \(L(u)/((1-u)(1-qu))\): a polynomial of zeros over a pair of poles. The bosonic trivial mode supplies the poles \(u=1\) and \(u=1/q\); the non-trivial bosonic modes are extra poles a curve does not have.

Why "super"? Take the logarithm: \[ \log Z_s(u) = \sum_{k\ge1}\frac{u^k}{k}\big(\operatorname{Tr}B^k - \operatorname{Tr}B_s^k\big) . \] Write \(N_k^{\rm even}\) and \(N_k^{\rm odd}\) for the numbers of closed non-backtracking walks of length \(k\) whose sign product is \(+1\) and \(-1\). Then \(\operatorname{Tr}B^k = N^{\rm even}_k + N^{\rm odd}_k\) and \(\operatorname{Tr}B_s^k = N^{\rm even}_k - N^{\rm odd}_k\), so \[ \operatorname{Tr}B^k - \operatorname{Tr}B_s^k = 2N^{\rm odd}_k \ \ge 0 . \] The difference of the two traces is a supertrace on the double cover (the trace of \(\tau\hat B^k\)), and it is an honest count: twice the number of closed walks of odd flux. Expanding each eigenvalue contribution, \[ 2N^{\rm odd}_k = \underbrace{q^k + 1}_{\text{trivial pair}} + \sum_{\text{bosonic, non-trivial}}\mu^k \;-\; \sum_{\text{fermionic}}\mu^k . \] The fermionic lines enter the count with a minus sign. That is the sign with which the zeros of Riemann's zeta enter the explicit formula for the primes, and the sign with which Frobenius eigenvalues enter the point count of a curve. The record's phrase for it: in the plain Ihara zeta the lines are bright (emitted by the counts); in the super zeta the fermionic lines are dark (absorbed), as for \(\zeta\) and for curves. There is also an Euler product, now only over prime cycles of odd flux, each carrying a boson and a fermion: \[ Z_s(u) = \prod_{C\ \text{prime, odd flux}} \frac{1+u^{\ell(C)}}{1-u^{\ell(C)}} . \]

A concrete coincidence to carry forward. For \(K_4\) with one negative edge the fermionic eigenvalues are \(\pm\sqrt5\) and \(\pm1\) (Demo 7). The mode \(\lambda = -1\) with \(q = 2\) has lines \(\mu = \tfrac12(-1\pm\sqrt{-7})\), and its factor \(1 + u + 2u^2\) is precisely the numerator of the zeta function of the elliptic curve \[ E:\ y^2 + xy = x^3 + 1 \ \text{ over } \mathbb F_2, \] which has 4 points over \(\mathbb F_2\) (the point at infinity, \((0,1)\), \((1,0)\), \((1,1)\)) and \(\#E(\mathbb F_{2^k}) = 2^k + 1 - (\mu^k + \bar\mu^k) = 4, 8, 4, 16, 44, 56, 116, 288, \dots\). We will call this fermionic mode the E mode. Section 14 shows that it is not a coincidence of numbers only: the mode carries a torus whose periodic points are these counts.

Weil positivity in the fermionic sector. The fermionic Weil form \(\mathcal Q_s = \begin{pmatrix}q&A_s/2\\A_s/2&1\end{pmatrix}\) has no trivial mode, so for a flux whose fermionic eigenvalues all lie in the band it is positive definite outright, with no pole plane to subtract. For \(K_4\) with one negative edge its smallest eigenvalue is \(\tfrac12\big(3 - \sqrt{1+5}\big) = 0.2753\). The prism with one negative edge around each ring (an "antiperiodic ring") is the counterexample: fermionic eigenvalues from \(-3\) to \(2.978\), five negative directions, RH false in this sector while all the counts \(2N^{\rm odd}_k\) remain nonnegative. Nonnegative counts alone do not give RH.

Keep

Flux \(\Rightarrow\) double cover \(\Rightarrow\) bosonic and fermionic sectors with matrices \(A\) and \(A_s\). The super zeta is zeros over poles; zeros from fermions, poles from bosons. Its supertrace is twice the count of odd-flux closed walks, in which fermionic lines are subtracted: the sign structure of curves and of \(\zeta\).

Part C · 11RH on average over all fluxes: the matching polynomial

A single flux may or may not be Ramanujan. Averaging over all fluxes gives a theorem. The fermionic characteristic polynomial \(\det(x - A_s)\), averaged over the \(2^g\) fluxes, is the matching polynomial of the graph (Godsil–Gutman): \[ \frac{1}{2^g}\sum_{\text{fluxes}}\det(x - A_s) = \sum_{k\ge0}(-1)^k m_k\,x^{\,n-2k}, \] where \(m_k\) is the number of ways to choose \(k\) edges with no shared vertex (a \(k\)-matching). The reason is elementary: expanding the determinant as a sum over permutations, every cycle of length three or more in a permutation picks up the product of the signs around that cycle, which averages to zero over fluxes, so only permutations made of fixed points and transpositions survive, and a set of disjoint transpositions is a matching. And the roots of the matching polynomial of a \((q+1)\)-regular graph are real and lie strictly inside \((-2\sqrt q, 2\sqrt q)\) (Heilmann–Lieb, from the theory of the monomer–dimer gas). So:

The flux-averaged fermionic numerator has all its zeros on the critical circle, for every regular graph. RH holds on average over fluxes. This is the one positivity mechanism in Part C that is a theorem rather than a computation, and it is the mechanism behind the interlacing-families proof that bipartite Ramanujan graphs of every degree exist (Marcus–Spielman–Srivastava): some flux must be at least as good as the average. It proves RH for an average and for one unnamed flux, not for a given one.

For \(K_4\): six edges give \(m_1 = 6\) and the three perfect matchings give \(m_2 = 3\), so the average is \(x^4 - 6x^2 + 3\), with roots \(\pm2.334\) and \(\pm0.742\), inside \(\pm2.828\). Six of the eight fluxes of \(K_4\) are Ramanujan in the fermionic sector; the two that fail are the zero flux (where \(A_s = A\) has the eigenvalue 3) and the all-negative flux (eigenvalue \(-3\), the cube's bipartite mode).

Part D · 12Interlude: GKP states from scratch

Everything in Part D is about one quantum system: \(n\) harmonic oscillators, one per vertex, with position and momentum operators \(\hat x_i, \hat p_i\). We use units in which the displacement operators \(W(e) = \exp\big(i(e_p\cdot\hat x - e_x\cdot\hat p)\big)\), which shift the state by the phase-space vector \(e = (e_x, e_p)\), obey \[ W(e)\,W(f) = e^{2\pi i\,\Omega(e,f)}\,W(f)\,W(e),\qquad \Omega(e,f) = e_x\cdot f_p - e_p\cdot f_x . \] Two displacements commute exactly when the oriented area between them is an integer.

A GKP code (Gottesman–Kitaev–Preskill) is defined by a lattice \(L\) in phase space whose vectors all have integer mutual areas, so that the displacements \(W(\lambda)\), \(\lambda\in L\), commute. The code space is the set of states fixed by all of them. The dual lattice \(L^\perp = \{e : \Omega(\lambda, e)\in\mathbb Z \text{ for all } \lambda\in L\}\) contains \(L\), and the dimension of the code is \(\sqrt{[L^\perp : L]}\). Two cases matter here:

  • Qunaught. \(L = L^\perp = \mathbb Z^{2n}\) (the square lattice with our units). The code is one-dimensional: a single state, the ideal comb \(\sum_{m\in\mathbb Z^n}|x = m\rangle\), which is also a comb in momentum. It encodes nothing. It is the state the number theorist calls \(\Theta\): in the record, Riemann's theta function is the qunaught of the lattice of rational numbers inside the adeles.
  • Qubit. \(L = \mathbb Z\times2\mathbb Z\) for one mode. Then \(L^\perp = \tfrac12\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z\), the index is 4, and the code is a qubit. The displacements by \(L^\perp\) that are not in \(L\) commute with every stabiliser but act nontrivially: \((\tfrac12, 0)\) is the logical \(X\) and \((0,1)\) the logical \(Z\).

What the code does. An unknown displacement error \(W(e)\) changes the eigenvalue of each stabiliser \(W(\lambda)\) by the phase \(e^{2\pi i\Omega(\lambda,e)}\). Measuring all stabilisers therefore reveals \(e\) modulo \(L^\perp\): the syndrome is a point of the syndrome torus \(\mathbb R^{2n}/L^\perp\). The decoder shifts back by the nearest point of \(L^\perp\); if that point lies in \(L\) the state is restored, if it lies in \(L^\perp\setminus L\) a logical error has happened. For the qunaught, \(L^\perp = L = \mathbb Z^{2n}\): every displacement is detected modulo the lattice and nothing can be flipped, since nothing is encoded. The syndrome torus of \(n\) qunaughts is \(\mathbb R^{2n}/\mathbb Z^{2n}\), and that torus is where Section 14 happens.

Ideal combs have infinite energy. A physical GKP state is the comb with a Gaussian envelope and Gaussian teeth, and the error correction is then approximate. Nothing on this page needs the physical version; the lattice, the syndrome torus and the logical group are the structures the arithmetic uses.

Part D · 13Lattice state 1: the flux is a GKP qubit on the cycle lattice

Return to the graph. Choose a spanning tree (for \(K_4\) drawn with vertex 0 in the centre: the three spokes). The remaining \(g\) edges, the cotree (for \(K_4\): the outer triangle), each close one independent cycle. Orient each cotree edge. A closed walk crosses cotree edge \(j\) some net number of times \(x_j\in\mathbb Z\) (forward minus backward), and the vector \(x = (x_1,\dots,x_g)\in\mathbb Z^g\) is the walk's cycle class (its homology class). Walks with the same class wind around the same cycles the same net number of times.

Here is the picture that makes this a lattice state. Unroll the graph along all its cycles at once: this is the maximal abelian cover, an infinite periodic graph, a crystal in \(\mathbb R^g\) whose period lattice is \(\mathbb Z^g\). A closed walk of class \(x\) lifts to a walk in the crystal from the origin to the lattice point \(x\). Let \(W_k(x)\) be the number of closed non-backtracking walks of length \(k\) in class \(x\). The counts \(N_k = \sum_x W_k(x)\) are the overlap of the spreading walker with the comb \(\sum_x\delta_x\) on the period lattice.

Now add a general flux: a phase \(e^{i\varphi_j}\) on cotree edge \(j\). A walk of class \(x\) picks up \(e^{i\varphi\cdot x}\), so the flux-twisted count is \[ \operatorname{Tr}B_\varphi^{\,k} = \sum_{x\in\mathbb Z^g} e^{i\varphi\cdot x}\,W_k(x) , \] the overlap with the Bloch comb \(\sum_x e^{i\varphi\cdot x}\delta_x\): the qunaught comb on \(\mathbb Z^g\) displaced in momentum by \(\varphi\). This is a GKP dictionary:

A \(\mathbb Z_2\) flux is the half period: \(\varphi = \pi a\) with \(a\in\{0,1\}^g\), so the sign of a walk is \((-1)^{a\cdot x}\). The classes with \(a\cdot x\) even form a sublattice of index two in \(\mathbb Z^g\), and a sublattice of index two is the stabiliser lattice of a GKP qubit (Section 12, with the qubit lattice in \(g\) dimensions). Its logical states:

  • logical \(|{+}\rangle\): the comb over all of \(\mathbb Z^g\), the bosonic sector, overlap \(\operatorname{Tr}B^k\);
  • logical \(|{-}\rangle\): the comb with signs \((-1)^{a\cdot x}\), the fermionic sector, overlap \(\operatorname{Tr}B_s^k\);
  • logical \(|1\rangle\): the comb on the odd coset \(a\cdot x\) odd. The supertrace \(2N^{\rm odd}_k\) is twice the overlap with this state. That is why the super counts are counts.

What RH says here. The difference \(N^{\rm even}_k - N^{\rm odd}_k = \operatorname{Tr}B_s^k\) is the sum of the \(k\)-th powers of the fermionic lines plus the tree term, so the fermionic lines lie on the critical circle if and only if \[ \big|N^{\rm even}_k - N^{\rm odd}_k\big| \le 2n\,q^{k/2} + n(q-1)\qquad\text{for all } k. \] Walks of even and odd flux are equinumerous up to a square-root error. In qubit language: the walker's bias between the two logical values decays like \(q^{-k/2}\), the fastest rate the tree allows. Fermionic RH is optimal dephasing of the flux qubit by the walk. For \(K_4\) with one negative edge the bias is \(0.20\), \(5\times10^{-3}\), \(9\times10^{-5}\) at \(k = 10, 20, 30\).

Part D · 14Lattice state 2: the fermionic sector is a syndrome torus

The first lattice state lived at the level of counts. The second lives inside the fermionic phase space of Part B, and it is where the elliptic curve appears. Put the signed adjacency matrix into the step: \[ M_s = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -1\\ q & A_s\end{pmatrix},\qquad M_s^T\Omega M_s = q\,\Omega . \] Its entries are integers. So \(M_s\) maps the lattice \(\mathbb Z^{2n}\) into itself. By Section 12, \(\mathbb Z^{2n}\) is the stabiliser lattice of \(n\) qunaughts, one per vertex, and \(\mathbb R^{2n}/\mathbb Z^{2n}\) is their syndrome torus. The fermionic step acts on syndromes. (So does the bosonic step; what singles out the fermionic sector is the sign with which its lines enter the counts, and the absence of the trivial mode.)

Three facts, each elementary:

  1. Points are periodic syndromes. A syndrome \(e\) on the torus has period \(k\) if \(M_s^ke \equiv e \pmod{\mathbb Z^{2n}}\). The number of such \(e\) is the number of integer points of the parallelepiped spanned by the columns of \(M_s^k - 1\), which is \[ h_k = \big|\det(1 - M_s^k)\big| = \prod_{\text{fermionic lines}}\big(1-\mu^k\big). \]
  2. The cohomology of the torus is the fermionic Fock space. The \(j\)-th cohomology of a \(2n\)-torus is the \(j\)-th exterior power of \(\mathbb R^{2n}\): the states of \(j\) fermions filling modes of phase space. Expanding the determinant is the Lefschetz fixed-point formula, \[ \det(1-M_s^k) = \sum_{j=0}^{2n}(-1)^j\operatorname{Tr}\Lambda^jM_s^k : \] empty state, minus one-fermion states, plus two-fermion states, and so on. The zeros of the super zeta are the eigenvalues of the step on the one-fermion states.
  3. For one fermionic mode the two zeta functions coincide. With \(n=1\), \(\det(1-M^k) = 1 - \operatorname{Tr}M^k + q^k\): the empty state, the two one-fermion states \(\mu^k, \bar\mu^k\) with a minus sign, and the filled state \(q^k\). For the E mode (\(q=2\), \(\lambda=-1\)) this is \(2^k + 1 - (\mu^k+\bar\mu^k) = 4, 8, 4, 16, 44, 56, \dots\): the periodic syndromes of one GKP mode are the points of \(E\) over \(\mathbb F_{2^k}\), and the zeta function of this map on the torus, \(\exp\sum_k h_ku^k/k\), is \(\dfrac{1+u+2u^2}{(1-u)(1-2u)}\), the zeta function of \(E\). The two poles are the empty and the filled state.
Demo 11Periodic syndromes of one GKP modeThe unit square is the syndrome torus. Dots are the syndromes of period \(k\), coloured by orbit under the step. "Apply the step" moves a cloud of syndromes to show the map.

For several fermionic modes the super zeta keeps only the one-fermion states, while the torus counts the whole Fock space; the two differ the way a curve differs from its Jacobian. What survives in general: the step-invariant syndromes (\(k=1\)) form a finite abelian group of order \(\det(1-M_s) = \det\big((q+1)-A_s\big)\), the determinant of the signed Laplacian. For \(K_4\) with one negative edge it is \(\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_8\), of order 32; for Petersen with its dodecahedral flux, order 5184. The record reads this group as the analogue of the rational points of a Prym variety (the odd part of a double cover of curves); that is an analogy, not a theorem.

Part D · 15The tower of codes, and what it does not see

The periodic syndromes organise themselves into GKP codes. Write \(V = qM_s^{-1}\), also an integer matrix (it is the adjoint of \(M_s\) for \(\Omega\), with \(M_sV = q\)). For each \(k\) the sublattice \(L_k = (1-V^k)\,\mathbb Z^{2n}\) has index \(h_k\) in \(\mathbb Z^{2n}\), and the GKP code \(\mathcal C_k\) it defines has:

  • basis: the qunaught displaced by each syndrome of period \(k\), \(W(e)|L\rangle\); distinct periodic syndromes give orthogonal states;
  • dimension \(h_k\), the number of points over \(\mathbb F_{q^k}\); and \(\mathcal C_d\subset\mathcal C_k\) when \(d\mid k\), so a field extension is a code extension;
  • logical Paulis: displacements by periodic syndromes are the logical shifts, the stabilisers of \(\mathbb Z^{2n}\) modulo \(L_k\) the logical phases; together, the Heisenberg group of the finite group of points;
  • the step as a logical Clifford: \(W(e)|L\rangle\mapsto W(M_se)|L\rangle\) permutes the basis and preserves the commutation pairing. On \(\mathcal C_k\) its order divides \(k\): it is the Galois group of \(\mathbb F_{q^k}\) over \(\mathbb F_q\), acting by Cliffords. Its orbits on the basis are the places: an orbit of size \(d\) is a point of degree \(d\).

The two sides of the zeta function are two traces of the same step: on the code, \(\operatorname{Tr}(\Phi^j|_{\mathcal C_k}) = h_{\gcd(j,k)}\), a unitary permuting a basis, which gives the Euler product (the cycle decomposition of a permutation) and is positive for free; on the Fock space, the supertrace \(\sum_j(-1)^j\operatorname{Tr}\Lambda^jM_s^k\), where the zeros live. Lefschetz says the two agree.

The tower is blind to RH. Everything in the list above exists for any integer step with \(M^T\Omega M = q\Omega\). The record's test case is the hyperbolic step \(\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\5&5\end{pmatrix}\), with real eigenvalues \(3.618\) and \(1.382\), so RH is false for it; it still has codes of dimensions \(1, 11, 76, 451, 2501\), logical Heisenberg groups, a Galois Clifford, places. What differs is only a ratio: RH says \(h_k/q^{nk}\to1\) with error \((1+q^{-k/2})^{2n}-1\), and the hyperbolic step leaves that window from \(k=2\) on. The tower is kinematics; positivity is not in it.

Part D · 16Where RH lives: an invariant vacuum

Normalise the step: \(S = M_s/\sqrt q\) is symplectic, a Gaussian unitary on \(n\) modes. Three statements are equivalent (this is a standard fact about symplectic matrices; the notebook has its abstract form as a theorem on invariant polarisations):

  1. every eigenvalue of \(M_s\) has modulus \(\sqrt q\), and \(M_s\) is diagonalisable (RH with semisimplicity);
  2. the fermionic Weil form \(\tfrac12\Omega(M_s - V)\) is positive definite;
  3. there is a pure Gaussian state that \(S\) leaves invariant: a vacuum. Concretely, a complex structure \(J\) on phase space, \(J^2=-1\), with \(\Omega(\cdot, J\cdot) > 0\) and \(JS = SJ\).

With the vacuum in hand everything becomes oscillators. In the vacuum's normal modes \(S\) is a rotation by an angle \(\theta_j\) in the \(j\)-th mode, so its Gaussian unitary is one period of \(n\) harmonic oscillators, \(\exp\big(-i\sum_j\theta_j(\hat n_j+\tfrac12)\big)\). The zeros are oscillator frequencies: \(\mu_j = \sqrt q\,e^{i\theta_j}\). The Hilbert–Pólya operator is the oscillator Hamiltonian, and its positivity is Weil positivity. For \(K_4\) with one negative edge the four frequencies are \(\theta/\pi = 0.210, 0.385, 0.615, 0.790\). In the bosonic sector the trivial mode is different: there \(S\) is a pure squeeze by \(\sqrt q\), generated by \(\tfrac12(\hat x\hat p+\hat p\hat x)\), an inverted oscillator. A step that violates RH has more inverted oscillators.

The lattice gets a shape. For one mode, the coordinate \(z = \bar\mu\,x + p\) turns the step into multiplication by the complex number \(\mu\): \(z\mapsto\mu z\). (Check: \((x,p)\mapsto(-p, qx+\lambda p)\) gives \(z' = -\bar\mu p + qx + \lambda p = \mu\bar\mu x + (\lambda-\bar\mu)p = \mu z\), using \(\mu\bar\mu = q\) and \(\mu+\bar\mu = \lambda\).) The stabiliser lattice \(\mathbb Z^2\) becomes a lattice in the complex plane with complex multiplication: the map \(z\mapsto\mu z\), a rotation by \(\theta\) and a dilation by \(\sqrt q\), sends the lattice into itself. The invariant quadratic form on it is \(qx^2+\lambda xp + p^2\) of discriminant \(\lambda^2 - 4q\). For the E mode this is the lattice \(\mathbb Z\big[\tfrac{1+\sqrt{-7}}2\big]\); for the Pauli-six mode (\(q=5\), \(\lambda=-2\)) it is the rectangle \(\mathbb Z + 2i\mathbb Z\). For the hyperbolic step the discriminant is \(+5\), the form is indefinite, and there is no such picture at all: RH for one mode is the statement that the discriminant is negative, so that the lattice is a lattice in the complex plane.

And then every level of the tower is a scaled, rotated copy of the lattice: the point-tower lattices \((1-M^k)\mathbb Z^2\) and the kernel-tower lattices \(M^k\mathbb Z^2\) are \((1-\mu^k)L\) and \(\mu^kL\). The tower is self-similar. Without a vacuum there is no such statement.

Demo 13The lattice in vacuum coordinates, and the step as a rotation\(z = \bar\mu x + p\) for \((x,p)\in\mathbb Z^2\). Press "apply the step" to see \(z\mapsto\mu z\) carry the lattice onto the sublattice \(\mu L\) (shown in teal). Hollow dots: \((1-\mu)L\).

In the ordinary case this is a theorem. A free \(\mathbb Z\)-module with an endomorphism \(F\) satisfying (1) \(F\) semisimple with all eigenvalues of modulus \(\sqrt q\), (2) half the eigenvalues \(p\)-adic units and half divisible by \(q\), (3) the middle coefficient of its characteristic polynomial prime to \(p\), (4) an integer \(V\) with \(FV = q\), is a Deligne module, and Deligne's theorem says these are exactly the ordinary abelian varieties over \(\mathbb F_q\). Our \((\mathbb Z^{2n}, M_s)\) always has (4); (1) is RH; (3) is ordinariness and implies (2) when \(q\) is prime. The E mode (\(x^2+x+2\)), the Pauli-six mode (\(x^2+2x+5\)) and \(K_4\) with one negative edge (\(x^8+2x^6+5x^4+8x^2+16\), rank 8) all pass, so each fermionic sector is an ordinary abelian variety over \(\mathbb F_q\), of dimension 1, 1 and 4, with the GKP lattice as the first homology of its canonical lift and the level-\(k\) code as functions on its \(\mathbb F_{q^k}\)-points. Petersen and \(K_5\) with their in-band fluxes have the eigenvalue 0 and are not ordinary. Whether the vacuum's complex structure is the lift's complex structure is a separate sign question, settled in the record's later pages (howe-positivity.md, sign-vector.md): yes for one mode, no for \(K_4\) with one negative edge, where a twisted form supplies the polarisation instead.

Keep

Tower, codes, logical Paulis and the Galois Clifford exist with or without RH. The datum that carries RH is a vacuum invariant under the normalised step, which lives on phase space (first homology), not on the space the points live in. In the understood case the zeros are oscillator frequencies and the squeeze belongs to the poles. The record's steer: carry the vacuum, not the tower, to the case of Riemann's zeta.

Appendix · 18Glossary

\((q+1)\)-regular graph
Every vertex has \(q+1\) neighbours. \(q\) plays the role of the size of the finite field.
Non-backtracking walk
A walk that never immediately reverses the edge it just used. Closed ones (also non-backtracking at the closing step) are the periodic orbits.
Hashimoto operator \(B\)
The \(0/1\) matrix on directed edges that moves a walker one non-backtracking step. \(N_k=\operatorname{Tr}B^k\).
Ihara zeta
\(\prod_C(1-u^{\ell(C)})^{-1}=1/\det(1-uB)\) over prime cycles \(C\).
Ramanujan graph
All non-trivial adjacency eigenvalues satisfy \(|\lambda|\le2\sqrt q\). Equivalent to RH for the Ihara zeta.
Lines
The eigenvalues \(\mu\) of the step \(M\), two per adjacency eigenvalue, \(\mu^2-\lambda\mu+q=0\). RH: \(|\mu|=\sqrt q\) off the trivial mode.
Symplectic form \(\Omega\)
The oriented area \(x\cdot p'-p\cdot x'\) on phase space. \(M\) scales it by \(q\); \(S=M/\sqrt q\) preserves it.
Weil form \(\mathcal Q\)
The quadratic form conserved by the step; \(q|x|^2+x\cdot Ap+|p|^2\). Its positivity off the trivial mode is RH.
Flux
A choice of signs on the edges modulo gauge changes at vertices; equivalently a double cover; \(2^g\) of them.
Bosonic / fermionic sector
Even / odd functions on the double cover; adjacency \(A\) / signed adjacency \(A_s\).
Super zeta
\(\det(1-uB_s)/\det(1-uB)\). Zeros from fermions, poles from bosons.
Supertrace
\(\operatorname{Tr}B^k-\operatorname{Tr}B_s^k=2N^{\rm odd}_k\), the trace weighted by the deck involution of the cover.
Matching polynomial
\(\sum_k(-1)^km_kx^{n-2k}\), \(m_k\) the number of \(k\)-edge matchings; the flux average of \(\det(x-A_s)\).
GKP code
The joint fixed space of the displacement operators of a lattice \(L\) with integer mutual areas; dimension \(\sqrt{[L^\perp:L]}\).
Qunaught
A GKP code of dimension one (\(L=L^\perp\)); a single comb state that encodes nothing and detects every displacement modulo \(L\).
Syndrome torus
\(\mathbb R^{2n}/L^\perp\), what the stabiliser measurements reveal about a displacement.
Cycle lattice
\(\mathbb Z^g\) of cycle classes (first homology); the position lattice of the flux qubit.
Period-\(k\) syndrome
\(e\) with \(M_s^ke\equiv e\) mod \(\mathbb Z^{2n}\); their number is \(h_k=\det(1-M_s^k)\), the point count over \(\mathbb F_{q^k}\).
Vacuum
A pure Gaussian state, i.e. a complex structure \(J\) with \(\Omega(\cdot,J\cdot)>0\). RH \(\iff\) one is invariant under the normalised step.
Deligne module
\((\mathbb Z^{2n},F)\) with RH, ordinariness and an adjoint \(V\); the same thing as an ordinary abelian variety over \(\mathbb F_q\).
