                                     Introduction
   In this paper, we study how varying the parameters in the cubic-theta argument of
[Ope26a] improves its zero-free half-plane. After recalling the original argument, we derive
three successive improvements and determine the optimal boundary within the specified
system of estimates.
   The quasi-Riemann hypothesis asks whether there is a fixed B < 1 such that ζ(s) ̸= 0
for Re s > B, with its pole at s = 1 allowed. The uniform character version asks for the
same B independently of conductor and height. A fixed half-plane supplies a uniform gap
between zeros and the line Re s = 1; classical zero-free regions instead narrow towards
that line as conductor or height increases. For general analytic background, see [IK04,
Chapter 5]; for the Dedekind zeta function of an abelian extension, and hence its Hecke
L-function factors, a precise zero-free region allowing an exceptional real zero is [TZ19,
Theorem 3.1]. Zero-density estimates bound the number of zeros in a region; for example,
see [GM26, Theorem 1.2].
   We follow the 30 September 2026 preprint [Ope26a], retaining its numbering in all cita-
tions to it. The companion [Ope26b] gives a different 11/12 argument. OpenAI’s public
math repository has a formalization scope note [Ope26c, Scope] that lists the 7/8 state-
ments for zeta, Dirichlet and finite-order Hecke functions, with principal poles excluded;
the paper’s later applications are outside that scope.

   All three improvements retain the exact arithmetic formulas and principal residues of
[Ope26a]. Their Euler correction is shown to be holomorphic below 7/8. In Parts II
and III, the original conclusion β∗ ≤ 7/8 permits κ = 3/4, removing the loss associated

   Here is the structure of the paper. Part I recalls the 7/8 argument and fixes the arith-
metic and analytic notation used throughout. Part II establishes the shared changed-
parameter estimates and gives the simpler rational extension Br . Its calculation in the
Bernstein basis polynomials is retained as a separate rational example; this bound is su-
perseded by the stronger one in Part III. Part III varies both length parameters, proves
the error-exponent inequality at the algebraic parameter choices, and discusses the result-

the changed count and length parameters, and iterates the analytic comparison to Bnew .
All three extensions use Proposition 3.1 and the Dirichlet transfer of Corollary 14.1.

Part I. Summary of the 7/8 proof
           1. The original argument and its two-stage reduction
   In this section, we briefly describe the two representations of the cubic-theta average,
i.e., the smoothed arithmetic average Iη (X, Y, Z) defined in (4.5), formed from the Fourier
coefficients of the cubic theta function, and the two-stage reduction leading to the 7/8
bound. The first stage proves 11/12 using the unmodified average; the second introduces
selected prime factors and stronger moment estimates to reach 7/8.
   The argument compares two descriptions of one cubic-theta average: the divisibility-
restricted form of the cubic-theta functional equation (4.2) ([Ope26a, Corollary 14.1])
gives the theta mean-square estimate (7.4). Together with the additive mean-square es-
timate (7.5), the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (4.7) gives the direct bound (7.3). Poisson
summation (4.9), applied to the modification (7.2), isolates the principal Mellin integral
(8.8) involving 1/LF (s, η). The simultaneous large values (5.5) forced by a zero, together
with the moment bounds (9.1) and (9.4), control the remaining Poisson terms. Part I
makes those dependencies, the common-character hypotheses and the numerical expo-
nent comparisons explicit. Its statements record the original argument using the proved
results indexed in Appendix B. The accompanying explanations retain the decisive identi-
ties, exponent comparisons and contradiction, including calculations used in Parts II–IV.
Complete proofs of the imported results are given at the cited locations in [Ope26a]. Num-
bered statements have this paper’s numbering; locators inside citations always refer to
the cited work.
